### 1.1 Building sector facing climate change

In 2021 the buildings were responsible for 30% of global final energy consumed. That pro-
duced 26% of the CO2 emissions coming from this energy sector (Delmastro et al., 2023). In the
same year, in Europe, there was a rebound in the energy consumption in buildings with an esti-
mated 4.8% of increment according to the European Environment Agency. Then, the majority of
the floor area is composed by residential buildings in the European Union. In France 76% of the
building floor area, are residential buildings (Camarasa et al., 2018).
Regardless the energy conservation efforts made by developed countries, the energy demand
for buildings increased more than 20% between 2000-2017. This is the result of increasing floor
area and population and limited improvements in energy efficiency. Direct CO2 (3GtCO2) emis-
sions are a result of fossil fuels combustion to satisfy thermal building needs, water heating and
cooking. Consumption of electricity was responsible of 9.8GtCO2 (Santamouris and Vasilako-
poulou, 2021). Moreover trends indicate that the world energy consumption will grow by 56%
between 2010 and 2040.

### 1.2 Increasingly high-performance technologies in terms of comfort,

but often with high energy impacts despite advances in efficiency

For years there existed the vernacular architecture, which adapted to climate conditions and
took advantage of radiation, ventilation and humidification, among others. Vernacular architecture
is therefore one of the bases of what we know today as bioclimatic design. It is a guideline (from
the past) for futuristic construction, due to its environmentalist approach (Manas, 2010).
The utilisation of vernacular architecture decreased when new technologies were introduced
in the industrial revolution times. Heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC) technologies
boomed when the need to heat up and cool down factories and large buildings increased. Heating
steam radiators were created in 1820 using a central boiler and a network of pipes and radiators
that distributed the steam through the building. Then the first air conditioning system used ice to
cool air and it was created to help reducing infectious deceases in hospitals, by John Gorrie in
1851. Later in 1902 when the electricity developed, Wills Carrier invented the first modern air
conditioner. It was created to remove moisture from the air, but he noticed that it cooled down
the air at the same time. While the technology improved the size of the invention became smaller.
Then by 1931 the window AC unit was created. The technology that is used nowadays was based
on this model. From then the usage of AC systems scaled and started to be used in residential,
commercial and industrial buildings. Today HVAC systems can control not only temperature, but
humidity and air quality (Mckoy et al., 2023).
Buildings include occupants and they also have an impact in the process of energy manage-
ment. When HVAC sytems appeared, people could modify their behaviour in a way that they avoid
discomfort or dissatisfaction, i.e. the services of a building would be adapted to the preferences
of the occupants. Being able to change environmental conditions such as the level of heating or
the ventilation. Adaptive opportunity is applicable to thermal, acoustical, air quality, vibration and
visual and lighting comfort (Yao, 2013). However, the requirements of comfort have an important
environmental and economical impact.
(Bozsaky, 2010) takes us to a journey in the history of insulation. explaining the different
materials used in different times. He also explains the way they were discovered and methods
to generate them. In the beginning the term insulation material did not even exist. Nomads in
2.5 mill - 7000BC built (non lasting) homes using the same materials they used for clothes such

as animal skin, fur, wool or plants like reed, flax or straw. They intended to protect themselves
from wild animals, and low or high temperatures. It was after developing the agriculture that they
created more lasting dwellings using earth or they lived in caves. Using soil kept the heat in the
winter and provides a fresh internal atmosphere in the summer, nowadays this is called thermal
lag. Other materials were used from nature such as wood, straw, eel grass and reed. Later during
the industrial revolution, materials such as cast-iron, concrete and steel started to be used, but the
thermal insulation of this materials was lower than the adobe bricks, besides cracks were caused
due to thermal expansion.
In 1880 calculations on heat loss and heat gain started to be developed and focus was on
lowering losses on thermal power plants (due to high costs of fossil fuels and the long depression
between 1873-1896, one of the main reasons to develop thermal insulation), keeping the houses
warm and the households comfortable. Thermal insulation started with the use of natural materials
such as reed panels, which were highly flammable, later in 1920 baggage with asbestos cement
(a mineral of rocks and soil) was tested to reduce the flammability. Asbesto was also used to
insulate engines, boilers and chimneys, but this material was proved to be harmful for health and
even proved to caused deaths in 1906. Other mineral wool products were tested turning lava into
fibre, but their production was dangerous. In 1938 the first fibers of glass were used as insulation
material. Between 1870-1950 hollow bricks appeared and have improved on their thermal and
mechanical strength properties. Aerated autoclaved concrete (AAC), a material resistant to fire,
mold and to rot took 5 years to be developed, until 1923. Loose insulation appeared as well with
the use of perlite in 1950. At the same time one of the most used material nowadays came up,
the plastic foams (polystyrene and polyurethane), after 1970 it was the most reproduced materials
for thermal insulation. In 1943, ten years after the first polystyrene foam was made, it was put on
the market. In 1950 polyurethane foam was used for aircraft’s coating, during world war II and
in 1954 it started to be commercialized. Nowadays, mineral wool is the first material used, then
the plastic foams and lastly the natural materials. After realizing that fossil fuels are not renewable
and considering that the synthesis of non natural materials have a strong environmental and energy
impact, studies have been made to use again the natural materials such as cork, wood wool, sheep
wool and new materials such as vacuum insulation panels (Bozsaky, 2010).
In 1883 when the industrial revolution developed solutions for transportation, production and
agriculture, while daily household chores such as cleaning and cooking could take up to 40h a
week. If inhabitants had a stable economy, staff was hired to help with the house work. In 1886
Josephine Crochane wondered why a machine to wash the dishes had not been invented. This ac-
tivity was maybe not considered important for the productivity as other machines were necessary
in the industries. Cochrane decided to invent a washing machine based on water pressure and not
scrubbers to avoid breaking the dishes. Although it was expensive for the house usage, restaurants
and hotels were interested in a machine that could save time and money, so in 1898 she had her
own factory, but it was until after wold war II that they became affordable and electric, for people
to use them at home. Washing clothes was a long task such as washing dishes. Washing clothes
could take 1 full day, since it used to be made by hand. In 1907 Frederick Maytag, who used to
work for a farm equipment business, decided to develop a washing machine that could be operated
by hand. Later in 1922 the first aluminum tube that agitated clothes, could operate with electri-
city or gasoline (as the current washing machine) (Mcgee, 2024). Nowadays the Maytag company
is one of the greatest producers of washing machines. In 1869 Ives McGaffey patented a carpet
sweeper that worked by hand moving a crank that generated suction. Later this machines worked
with gasoline. James Murray Spangler created a vacuum cleaner that worked with electricity. He
received a patent for it in 1908 (Mcgee, 2024). William Hoover invested in the vacuum cleaner
business. Today the Hoover company is a multi-million dollar business (National inventors hall of
fame). The refrigerator was invented due to, health causes, food was contaminated in its transpor-
tation from farms to homes. In 1915 Alfred Mellowes created the first refrigerator for homes, after
trials with compressed gases (refrigerants) were used on a commercial scale. As it can be seen, the

household appliances were born from needs to save time and reduce efforts for the development

Goods and services were divided as time-saving or time-using. The time-saving goods redu-
ced the time taken for a task, such as the washing machine. The time-using goods require time of
interaction with the product "to produce satisfaction", such as the TV. Some affirm that compa-

cultural norms and increasing "standards of house care". "Washing machines did not save time,

Some appliances diffused faster than others, for instance entertainment appliances like radio
and TV were more easily acquired than household appliances such as washing machines and re-
frigerators. Non the less all the appliances diffusion had a rising trend. In the US people started
watching 2h and 22 minutes of TV and in UK 2h and 14 min per day. Time-using technology
has also been more innovative than time-saving technology, radio was replaced by TV in black
and white and then this one was replaced by colored TV (Bowden and Offer, 1994). Other hou-
sehold appliances have had advances too, but to improve the energy efficiency. In refrigerators,
for example, improvements have been made in insulation, the use of new motors, high efficiency
compressors, heat exchangers, etc. Also gases such as the CFCs (chlorofluorocarbon gases) have
been reduced after the Montreal Protocol in 1990(Environment, 2018). Clothes washers can for
example determine the amount of water, detergent and temperature for specific types of clothes.
This has resulted in 20 percent of energy and water reduction. Microprocessor based sensors are
also installed in a variety of appliances like door open alarm sensor in refrigerators, clothes load
sensors in clothes washers, remote access to TV and in general smart grid operations. Controlling
remotely the appliances in dwellings can help by turning on the appliances in not peak time de-
mand or to start appliances before arriving if needed like the heater in winter or turning off lights
when there is no movement detected. Saving energy in demand times could be the biggest potential
of this technology, but the different interested parties as electricity generators, network companies,
appliance manufacturers the inhabitants, etc. would need to join efforts to actually make it work
(Bansal et al., 2011).
Through the time, humanity has progressed on the systems we use to solve our needs : to
protect us from the weather and the animals, systems and machines to make us more productive
or to entertain us, etc. but, further more, we have improved systems to consume less energy while
providing better quality services. So why energy consumption is still an issue ? Why the need of
the energy management ? Is it just because population is increasing ? Is it maybe because despite
the progress in our systems, if they are used incorrectly, we end up wasting energy ?

#### 1.2.1 The energy consumption is more sensible to human behaviour in new and

more efficient buildings
In the majority of EU countries, half of the residential buildings were constructed before 1970,
when thermal regulations started to appear (European Commission, 2013). Thermal regulations
are a strategy to promote energy efficiency in buildings. They are composed of a set of legal and
compulsory requirements that include specifications on thermal properties of construction and
insulation materials, windows, HVAC, lighting, electrical power and renewable energies integra-
tion. Efforts have been done to increase the efficiency in buildings, by renovating and using better
materials and technologies (Allouhi et al., 2015).
In France the first Thermal Regulation (RT by its name in french : Réglamentation Thermique)
appeared in 1974, not with environmental intentions, but due to an oil shock. The economy and
aiming at an energy independence were the main reasons to develop the regulations. In 1974,

−25% of energy consumption was the objective. This was achieved due to thermal insulation
and the management of mechanical ventilation. In 1998 the RT is also applied to non residential
buildings and searches for costs optimization. Cooling and heating needs were considered, but
also energy efficiency of equipment’s such as boilers, to reduce investment in insulation. In the
RT2000 the usage of opening and closing of windows and thermal inertia is privileged. In 2002
the Energy Performance of Buildings Directive (EPBD) require all members of the EU to have
buildings regulations (Allouhi et al., 2015).
By 2005 when a new RT is proposed, the bioclimatic design is emphasized (a new word to call
practices of the previous to industrial revolution architecture). The use of renewable energies as
solar panels was also introduced. For the RT2012 results, analysis of the buildings and standards
are requested. In the last regulation, the RE2020 envisions that the building produces more energy
that what it consumes : the concept of positive energy buildings (Boesch, 2020). The recent EPBD
also required all new buildings to be nearly zero-energy by the end of 2020 (Allouhi et al., 2015).
Note how the thermal regulation in 1974 solves the energy management issue by using "me-
chanical ventilation" and as the time passes it seems that more techniques of the times before the
industrial revolution (no mechanical systems) are requested in the regulations, to reduce energy
consumption.
A passive building design is really a net-zero-energy building and the local meteorological
conditions should be carefully considered. A main strategy of the passive systems is to prevent
heat loss in winter and reduce solar gains in summer. The main elements of passive design include
(Yao, 2013) :
1. Building orientation and window sizing and placing : The location and weather are consi-
dered to define this criteria in order to control solar gains, for example. Passive solar techniques
define the sunlight entrance, its storage and distribution into the building. Walls can be used to
store heat and shiny floors might reflect light. Finally shading strategies will intend to limit the
sunlight and heat entrance to the building, they can be external as part of the building or internal
shade elements such as curtains.
2. The built form : Shape, insulation, thermal response. By increasing the insulation, the ther-
mal transmittance U-value (W /m2 K) is reduced and heat/loss gain can be controlled which re-
duces, for example heating energy consumption in winter.
3. Natural ventilation : Techniques such as the cross ventilation, where there are openings on
two or more sides of a room, can be used to maximize airflow and therefore reduce heat. Another
technique is the stack ventilation in which the same temperature of air forces warm air to rise due
to its low pressure. Allowing fresh air from the outside into the building creating a buoyant effect.
In high-performance buildings, efficiency is strongly influenced by environmental factors such
as solar gain, wind exposure, temperature, etc. These impacts depend on a number of factors, such
as the building’s location, orientation, construction materials, shape and heating, ventilation and
air-conditioning system. There is a high influence of occupants on the final energy consumption
(Vorger, 2015).
(Peuportier et al., 2013) made the comparison of environmental impact indicators including
CO2, water, human health, energy consumption, etc. They showed that having a passive house can
reduce energy consumption, but having an energy sober behaviour rather a spendthrift behaviour
is, of course, better. Two users profiles were defined, economical and spendthrift in two house
cases : certified passive house and a reference house (this latter comprising the building requi-
rements in the French thermal regulation ). The results were shown as relative values using as
reference the worst case reference (house and spendthrift scenario). Although two houses might
not be representative of an average, in their calculations for both profiles, the energy use in KWh
by square meter per year was at least twice lower in the passive house. Energy used was considered
for heating, domestic hot water, cooking, lighting and appliances and ventilation. Also CO2 emis-
sions were reduced by 40% in the reference house case with a less consuming behaviour and by
80% for the best case (passive house and economical behaviour). They propose combining efforts :

Windows are the least resistant to heat transfer. 30% of the heat loss in winter is due to the
windows, similarly is through the window that solar gains are received into a building (cengel and
Ghajar, 2015).
Windows also provide light during the day and allow for ventilation. Double or triple-pane
windows can be used to minimize heat loss in winter and internal or external shading systems can
be used in summer to limit solar gains. When sun light is in contact with a glass surface, one part
of the energy is transmitted, reflected and absorbed. In a clear glass of 6mm thick 80% of the solar
radiation is transmitted, 12% absorbed, and 8% is reflected (cengel and Ghajar, 2015).
In zones with big glassing exposure, the mean radiant temperature (MRT) is important to
consider. The MRT is a function of position in which the radiant heat exchange between the human
body and its surroundings is computed. Humans gain or loss heat through radiation besides with
the air. It considers the temperature of each surface around and the view factor of each surface
representing the part of its radiation that reaches the person (SimScale, 2024). In an air conditioned
environment with a fixed percentage of relative humidity and negligible air movement, the comfort
would be only affected by the air temperature and MRT. To make calculations the Operative (Top)
has been defined as the mean between air and MRT.

(Batier, 2015) base their analysis in the adaptive comfort theory, applied in non air condi-
tioned buildings or when this is turned off. Comfort is achieved in temperatures higher than in
air conditioned spaces. Therefore the comfort is reached by opening and closing windows, for
example (Thellier, 2012) : "If occupants experience discomfort, they will react to restore their
comfort". (Batier, 2015) established "discomfort" based on temperatures and an equation to cal-
culate a comfort temperature based on the European regulation of 2007 (AFNOR NF EN 15251
Indoor environment criteria for the design and assessment of energy performance of buildings
covering indoor air quality, thermal, lighting and acoustics). They realised that the opening and
closing of windows and shading systems are mainly based on habits than to a reaction to discom-
fort. It was interesting to observe the effects of the actions and how they don’t necessarily give an
immediate comfort, but in the long term could be of benefit. Thanks to the measurements three
different profiles could be deduced : a counter-productive behaviour, an active behaviour and an
expert behavior. The counter-productive behaviour tends to open the windows in the afternoon
when external temperature is higher and shading systems are not used to protect from solar gains.
Besides the windows are closed in the night when the external temperature decreases. The active
behaviour corresponds to the occupant that modifies mainly the shading elements to reduce so-
lar gains. The expert behaviour corresponds to the occupant who left the shadings semi-opened
during the morning, then closed in the afternoon and opened in the night as well as the windows
evacuating the accumulated heat.
"To be acceptable to almost 90% of the population, the indoor operating temperature must be
within -3 and +3 degrees of the comfort temperature. In -3 degrees it is considered a discomfort
linked to cold, while in +3 the discomfort is linked to hot." The operative interior temperature was
calculated for the 11th and 12th june 2014 (days of the study), where the incomfort temperature
due to heat was calculated at around 28°C and the optimal comfort zone was around 25°C. The
researchers observed that the insulated dwelling in which shading systems and windows were
closed during the day and windows were opened during the night, kept a best comfort temperature
(Profile I-SN-3D-S), and after 3 days with a similar behaviour the temperature kept stable (+2°C).
The insulated dwelling which closed the shading systems but not the windows during the day and
closed the windows during the night, reached (on average) the limits of the comfort temperature
(Profile I-SN-2-A) and through 3 days the comfort temperature increased, overpassing the limit
of +3°C in the third day. Which resulted in +4°C. The non-insulated dwelling in which shading
systems were half closed, left the windows opened during the day and during the night (Profile
N-SN-2-S), was on average close to the limits of +3°C of the comfort temperature. As the time
passed, the comfort temperature also increased, but the third day started to overpass +4°C. Lastly
the worst profile (N-SN-1a-A) used no shading system during the day and also the windows were
left opened, while in the night the windows were closed. On average, from the midday the limit of
comfort temperature was over passed.

Outcomes from economic analysis demonstrated that it also exists a "rebound effect" resul-
ting from the reduced expenses by reducing energy consumption in more efficient buildings. The
amount of money saved by reducing energy consumption systems, can be used for example to
increase the purchases of new and more efficient services. For example, the installation of a heat
pump may reduce the operating cost to heat spaces, therefore, occupants can decide to use the
installed system for longer periods of time or they could use the money saved to install this tech-
nology in more rooms and to use it in higher temperatures and for longer periods of time. In a
wider perspective, the importance of the effect will be determined by the goods or services where
the money is re-spent. If it is used to travel or to buy local organic food, the environmental impact
will be different (Bourrelle, 2014), (Hens et al., 2010) and (A. Greening et al., 2000).
The way the systems are used can have a significant impact in the energy consumption. Inhabi-
tants might continue having a not energy sober behaviour even if the building has been modified to
be more efficient.There are variations in the energy consumption that are the result of human beha-
viour. The actions taken could be related to the intention of restoring comfort or for other reason,
as a habit, for example. Nonetheless when there is no improvement in the comfort of inhabitants,
and there is energy consumption is when wasteful behaviour takes place. Methods should be deve-
loped so that inhabitants can improve their behaviour towards a less consuming behavior, but also
that their needs can be fulfilled. Inhabitants should be able to understand how their systems and
services of their home, work and the impact they have according to the way they are used. In this
way energy efficient buildings will have a much better performance and "rebound effect" could be
avoided. More over, bad habits could be modified and inhabitants could bring this knowledge to
every building they visit or live in.

#### 1.2.2 Diversity in occupants behaviour

Behaviours in the residences are diverse. In (Firth et al., 2008), trends are identified in the
use of domestic appliances from household electricity consumption measurements. Researchers
mentioned that the main factors influencing the power consumption in domestic buildings are : the
number of appliances, its type, and the habits of the users. The diversity of this factors makes it
hard to predict accurately the power consumption. They published that the electricity consump-
tion of 72 houses increased in 2 years. The authors separated the electricity consumption in a
category of appliances : continuous appliances, standby appliances, cold appliances and active
appliances. The continuous appliances constantly consume energy like the burglar alarms. The
standby consumption is observed in, for example, televisions. This type of appliances are not
in active use, but they still consume energy. The cold appliances are the fridges and freezers,
they have mainly a cyclic consumption, not continuous, although they are in continuous use. The
active appliances were those without standby consumption, meaning that their switched off po-
wer consumption is zero. For this appliances it is clear if they are in use or not in use. They
mention that they observed very different results of power consumption across the 72 dwellings.
The dwelling that consumed the lowest energy in a year corresponded to 902kWh with a conti-
nuous and standby consumption of 104kWh, a cold appliance consumption of 236kWh and an
active appliance consumption of 562kWh. The highest annual total electricity consumption was of
7743kWh, with a continuous and standby consumption of 1993kWh, a cold appliance consump-
tion of 481kWh and active appliance consumption of 5269kWh. The difference between the lowest
and the highest annual total electricity consumption recorded was of 6841kWh. The dwellings
were separated in 3 groups according to their energy consumption. For the first group annual
consumption was between 902-2160kWh, for the second group 2174-3247kWh and for the third
group 3273-7743kWh. Cold appliance consumption was similar for the groups of low and medium
energy consumption groups and higher for the high energy group. The continuous and standby and
active consumption were greater in the high energy group than in the other 2. For all the dwel-
lings the continuous and stand by consumption represented 19% of the total consumption, 20%
represented the cold appliances and 61% the active appliance consumption.

#### 1.2.3 Including occupants impact in buildings’ modelling

The impact of the occupants in buildings, has also been identified through buildings simula-
tions and the differences observed between simulations results and the reality. Efforts have been
carried out to record occupants impact so that it is possible to include it in the simulations, no-
netheless the diversity of dwellings and occupants lifestyles make the task quite difficult. Energy
Buildings Simulation makes reference to software that allows to imitate a building’s energy per-
formance in a specific environment and with specific materials and geometry. Behind a graphical
interface where parameters can be modified, algorithms are executed making calculations related
to energy consumption based on location, orientation, materials, design, solar energy gains, natural
or mechanical ventilation and renewable energy installations such as heat pumps or solar panels
(Yao, 2013). The calculations are based on heat and mass balance equations showing results of the
conduction, convection and radiation as the interaction of the building with its surroundings (Chen
et al., 2022). Several parameters are customized to adapt the project as close as possible to the
reality. It also assesses in terms of costs and sustainability criteria such as life cycle analysis. Bet-
ter decisions can be made, since it allows to observe different scenarios, which otherwise would
be too costly to test if they were built. The aesthetic of the design and the compliance building
regulations can be evaluated with the same tool. There are over 400 building performance simula-
tion computer programs (Yao, 2013). Known tools are EnergyPlus, Dymola, DOE-2 (Chen et al.,
2022) and Pleiades (Vorger, 2015), for example.
Models that use building characteristics and calculations are often used to predict energy
consumption, but the results are limited as the model does not consider the impact of the nume-
rous different possibilities of the real occupants energy usage : occupant behavior, meaning that
the activities carried out in a daily basis have an impact on energy consumption (Bakaloglou and
Charlier, 2021). It has been shown that the behavior of human actors is one of the factors that has
the greatest impact on energy consumption in buildings (Vorger, 2015). (Delzendeh et al., 2017)
performed a literature review on the impact of occupants behaviours. They mention the importance
of study in this area due to the significant difference between the results of simulation tools to pre-
dict energy consumption and the evaluations post-construction. They mention that some of them
show a real energy consumption three times higher than the results of the simulation. In general
the occupants impact is desegregated from the simulations.
(Vorger, 2015) and (Happle et al., 2018) explain three approaches that are used to model oc-
cupancy in the buildings : the deterministic approach based on schedules, the approach based on
thermal comfort and the stochastic approach. In the deterministic approach, occupancy is mode-
led by fixed scenarios. Once they are stated, it is defined a unified behaviour for all occupants
leaving no room for variability in the behaviour. The thermal French regulation (RT 2012) states
standard schedules recovered from statistical studies for different activities sectors. (Hoes et al.,
2009) in (Ryan and Sanquist, 2012) propose using stochastic models and dividing occupants in
different categories, giving to each category an individual schedule. Although this methods are

more accurate, they require more time to collect data and compute the stochastic behaviours. No
interesting improvements have been carried out in the methods of models in this aspect, even when
improvements have been done during the years of their existence. In the mean time, large scale
data collections of occupants behaviour and further research in the use of stochastic models are
advised.
The approach based on statistical analysis will state a behaviour in terms of probabilities. At
specific conditions, people wont act all in the same way (as in the deterministic approach), but there
can be more probability of a certain behaviour. Although this approach is closer to the reality, many
information should be collected so that the behaviour can be representative. (Vorger, 2015) uses
this approach to simulate human behaviour using Pleiades for life events such as presence, usage
of appliances and actions on heating systems. Plaiades is a software for the simulation of buildings
and districts. Different calculations are possible such as thermal simulation, dynamic modeling,
french regulations verifications : RE2020, RT2012, RT global, ACV -C (énergie-carbone) and
DPE , air quality and HVAC systems sizing or statistical analysis (AMAPOLA). LCA analysis is
also included. Numerical architectural models can be transferred directly (Revit, gbXML or IFC).
Environmental specifications, materials and components can be selected from a data base library.
Several projects have been tested for dwellings and tertiary buildings for design and construction
or even buildings renovation (IZUBA, 2024).
(Vorger, 2015) used the Monte Carlo method, carrying out several simulations considering
the thermal model of the building and the behaviour model. He used data from surveys such
as Enquêtes d’Emploi du Temps (E.E.T.) carried out in France by the Insee in 1998-1999. The
survey contains information of 1544 people (810 kids) who belonged to 12000 houses. Some of
the demographic variables used in his studies were : day, sex, age, urban or rural location, type
of family (person alone, couple, couple and others and other option), employment status, etc. He
turned the information from the surveys into probability distribution figures.
For example to generate presence probability profiles, (Vorger, 2015) generated 2 models of
Markov : “order 1” and “elevated order”. For the first order the presence status of the next time
step is defined comparing the probability of change of status : probability of transition. For the
elevated order, the distribution of the duration of presence is also considered in function of the
characteristics of the person, the day of the week and the hour of arrival. The Markov chains are a
mathematical system used to explain transitions from one to other state using certain probabilistic
rules. The main characteristic of a Markov chain of first order is that the probability of transitioning
to any other state depends only on the present state. The state can be anything : transition of
presence-absence in this study. In other words, it is only necessary to know the previous state to
determine the probability distribution of the present state.
When generating the models, (Vorger, 2015) observed that the model of first order generated
profiles much closer to the median of the results of the survey, specially during the night hours.
It is mentioned in the investigation that an under estimation of the presence probability is less
problematic than an over estimation. Results that are shown by the first order curve. Also each of
the inhabitants is simulated in an independent way, then the model could over estimate the amount
of time in which there is presence, since the house has multiple inhabitants, in other words there
is an overlapping of the periods of presence of each individual.
Besides this, (Vorger, 2015) proposed a stochastic model of activities in buildings considering
demo graphical characteristics. In general it can be built individualised scenarios per occupant.
The opening and closing of windows and manipulation of shadows systems is included, as well
as a stochastic model for the usage of appliances, lightnings and hot and cold water. A heating
settings model was also developed using demographic characteristics and types of rooms, using
data from the UK and France, he found that the median temperatures are of 21°C, therefore 2°C
more than in the regulations scenarios. Like this for the series of simulations of different houses the
inhabitants had a set of characteristics and so several different scenarios and household diversity
were considered.

#### 1.2.4 From automatized Home Energy Management Systems to Empowering Oc-

cupants in Energy Smart Homes
In France 68% of electricity feeds residential and office buildings. The French survey institute
INSEE stated that people spend 90% of the day inside buildings. The (?) defines the occupant
behaviour as the "observable actions and reactions of a person in response to internal or external
stimuli, or actions and reactions to adapt to ambient environmental conditions (temperature, in-
door quality)". Energy related activities include heating, cooling, ventilation and window opening,
domestic hot water and electric appliances or lighting.
Decisions and habits at home are drivers of energy consumption. Making good decisions is not
evident since most of the phenomena are invisible : energy flux, air quality, temperature changes,
etc. Besides, the impacts depend on human practices which most of the time are routinized and
unconscious. Nowadays, sensors can be used to measure effects of human practices. This tech-
nologies are called Energy Management Systems (EMS) and when they have been developed for
domestic applications, this tools are better known as Home Energy Management Systems (HEMS).
They mix hardware and software that allows the occupants to monitor their energy consumption,
energy generation (when renewable energies are installed) , and energy exchange (if the a part
or the totality of the energy generated is to be sold or if energy needs to be purchased). Other
elements can be considered such as electric vehicles or energy storage systems. A HEMS utilises
automatic and communication systems. This includes (Balakrishnan and Geetha, 2021) :
1. Smart meters : Records energy consumption as they did previously the analog meters.
2. Sensing devices : Identifies phenomena and changes such as temperature, movement, CO2,
light or even energy consumption of specific appliances.
3. Communication devices : They help the meters and sensors to interact : ZigBee, LAN (Local
Area Network), Bluetooth, PAN (Personal Area Network), HAN (Home Area Network), NAN
(Neighbor Area Network) and WAN (Wide Area Network)
4. Appliances : Refers to home appliances they can be smart (their activation or deactivation
can be programmable or controlled remotely) or conventional.
The data collection is evolving towards a model predictive control (MPC) where the optimal
conditions are defined considering the user preferences, the exterior weather conditions and the
weather forecast, enabling to predict, for example, the internal temperature trends (Privara et al.,
2011). This technologies are still difficult to implement because of the variety of appliances in
buildings, which are not always controllable (Ploix et al., 2021). Model Predictive Control has
been studied also in energy communities for the management of consumption scheduling. When
a dwelling includes solar panels, batteries storage and also utilizes energy from the distribution
network (Albachrony, 2021). This methods have shown to have energy savings potential up to 15-
28% depending on factors such as insulation level and outside temperature. (Siroky et al., 2011)
Not only indoor conditions are planned to be predicted, but also the user behavior. Behavioral
control can result in 10-40% in energy savings for HVAC systems and 40% of the lighting elec-
tricity usage, but, that does not mean that the building is effective and comfortable. For example :
lights with sensors that turn off while the user is in the room can be “annoying”, or lights lasting
on for a while even if nobody is inside wont accomplish the purpose of energy efficiency (Nguyen
and Aiello, 2013).
An important factor to consider is the people and how they integrate the use of this technolo-
gies to their lifestyles as part of a building, because this is something new, different to what they
are used to live with in a building (Molinari and Kordas, ). So should also be thought how this
technology can be implemented in an “ergonomic” way so that they don’t represent a charge or a

problem in people´s life. In any smart system installed, the user should not be an expert to interact
with the different devices installed, but the systems should be easy for the user to utilize them
(Balta Ozkan et al., 2013). Moreover regardless the advances in technology it is important that the
inhabitants become part of the energy management system, rather than the technology controls the
services. If the controlled services don’t satisfy the needs the smart-tools may also end up barely
used or not used at all.
Technologies that provide feedback to the households, have been created so that we can better
understand the energy consumption effect of our habits. They track the energy usage patterns and
inform the users about it through for instance, mobile applications. The systems are an example
of Eco-feedback, a term used, when tools show a representation of the individual or collective
environmental impact in order to persuade people to reduce their consumption of resources such
as energy, water, food, etc. (Herrmann et al., 2021). Boulmaiz (2024) makes a reflection on per-
suasive systems applied to energy management. This systems intend to achieve people modifying
their behaviour towards a less energy consuming behaviour. Persuasive systems intend that people
believe they are the main decision makers, while they have been influenced. Persuasion can be
direct and explicit or indirect and implicit. The change of behaviour is not immediate, it is ra-
ther a process that needs time, therefore, persuasion and motivation need to be part of the process
until new habits have been achieved. Persuasion technologies have different functions to support
the change of behaviour, they can help to : understand, decide, react and protect. While the un-
derstanding function can include explanations and measures, the deciding function will provide
recommendations, or make simulations so that users can make a decision more easily. Then the
reactive function should act as a facilitator of the engagement to the task and finally the protective
function helps keeping motivation, and warning about undesired effects of the current behaviour.
(Prieto Gonzalez et al., 2021) mentioned in (Saad al sumaiti et al., 2014) the Entropy project
which aims to sensitize users about their energy consumption. They use functions of explana-
tion and recommendations. The dwellings are equipped with special sensors to collect the energy
consumption data. The tenants are informed and lifestyle changes are suggested to reduce their
energy consumption. Other projects presented the power consumption and the current electricity
price simultaneously to help users understanding the displayed values in case they were not aware
of how much a watt represents.
For instance, (Hariharan et al., 2021) proposed a system using the protective function. The
system can predicts the monthly electricity invoice. The users have access to the visualization of
the electricity usage and its corresponding monthly bill, this helps the users be conscious if they
are exceeding their energy consumption or not. The visualization and the energy consumption can
be controlled from an app. The method it uses to predict is a multiple linear regression algorithm.
They conclude that this proposal is efficient for reducing consumption purposes.
Other studies have tested non technologically approaches such as social networks for providing
energy feedback, applying persuasive interaction technologies with a social role. (Makivierikko
et al., 2019) mention that having a network feedback can influence local norms, encourage the
others or setting goals, but most importantly sharing comparative information or getting rewards
could work positively. Electricity cost could motivate, but the monetary savings from reducing
energy are not interesting compared with the lost of comfort. Even environmental concern has
proved to not be very effective. Providing feedback has not demonstrated a long term engagement
after some months of changes. To overcome this, social systems have been designed to cover
human needs such as the need to belonging to a group.
An interesting alternative would be to involve and empower electricity users, indicating to
them for example the convenient moments to make use of appliances when there are no peak loads,
or analyses of their practices with explanations and suggestions. Differently form the automatic
technologies, this approach would involve the energy consumers in the energy management system
(Ploix et al., 2021).

#### 1.2.5 Chapter conclusions

In this chapter it was introduced the evolution of buildings and the electrical appliances we
know nowadays, but it was emphasized how the inhabitants have an important impact in the energy
consumption.
Buildings protect us from the weather and its changes, keeping us in a comfortable environ-
ment. The different materials used for construction have evolved through the time with the dis-
coveries of their properties, availability and reproduction feasibility. Certain materials have even
been discovered to be harmful for the health. Regardless the material properties, in some regions,
the utilisation of heating and cooling services have been considered necessary, and today a variety
of systems are available for their installation in buildings to provide a more comfortable internal
environment.
What we know today as electrical appliances, provide services that reduce the time to carry
out certain tasks (time-saving appliances) such as the washing machine. Other appliances, such as
the TV provide other sort of "comfort" providing entertainment (time-consuming appliances). The
interaction with this appliances require of time and attention from the inhabitants. The appliances,
such as construction materials, have evolved. New components have been installed, the service
quality has been improved, energy sources have evolved and nowadays, the efforts are focused on
the energy efficiency of the appliances. Still the energy consumption is a subject of concern.
Thermal regulations in buildings demand more and more the construction of energy efficient
buildings. It has been observed that in this constructions, the energy consumption relies on the
inhabitants and their utilisation of the services : the occupants behaviour. The occupants usage

mentioned by (Thellier, 2012) "Buildings don’t use energy, people do !" It is for the inhabitants
that the services have been invented and "to respond to their comfort exigences" .
For the development of energy saving buildings, we have learnt to calculate and even simu-
late the thermal exchanges between the building and the environment and actually performing and
comfortable buildings have been constructed. But the energy consumed observed in the simula-
tions is much lower than in the reality due to the human factor and the difficulty of predicting
their behaviour. Tools have been developed to include the human behaviour in the calculations for
them to provide results as close as possible, to the reality. Although the stochastic methods can
provide an estimation, the only way to know the real consumption is by measuring it. Moreover,
the problem remains. Occupants can continue having a wasteful behaviour.
Thanks to the automation, some experts, have proposed to control the services of the buildings
to ensure a low and more efficient energy consumption. But automatized systems may not fulfill the
occupants needs. Besides each occupant might have different needs. For this the perfect automate
in this subject, might hardly exist. Too many factors to be programmed might need to be approved

by the inhabitants. A building is often built for it to last up to 50 years, but the introduction of new
technologies and the adaptation to them might take time, changes in the behaviour might be slow
as well. "It is unrealistic to aim for a more sober energy consumption without integrating buildings
users. The success can only be achieved with their contribution. Physicians and sociologists need
to work together to develop trans disciplinary studies" (Thellier, 2012)
So that inhabitants can be integrated they need to be able to understand their behaviour and its
impacts. Unfortunately the effects such as the energy consumption, air quality and temperature are
not visible. It is necessary to measure this effects, for that, environmental sensors can be used. The
next section explains methods that have been used to associate the human behaviour to measured
effects. One approach explains that human behaviour can be deduced from the measured effects,
the other approach requires of the participation of the inhabitants to add information about their
activities.
