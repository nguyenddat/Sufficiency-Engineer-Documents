# Introduction

[[Annotation aid system index|Index]] | [[00-article-information|Previous]] | [[02-related-works|Next]]

Most common role of smart buildings is to autonomously optimize energy consumption, comfort, health, and sustainability by continuously observing and reacting to their environment. Modern buildings are increasingly equipped with heterogeneous sensing infrastructures capable of monitoring temperature, humidity, CO2 concentration, occupancy, power consumption, water usage, and many other physical variables. Despite this abundance of sensing technologies, current smart buildings remain fundamentally limited because they can observe only the physical consequences of occupant behavior, not the reasons that generated them. A sensor may detect that a window has been opened, a dishwasher has started, or the heating setpoint has increased, but it cannot determine why these actions occurred. The occupant may have opened the window because the air felt stale, because someone was cooking, because of an unpleasant odor, or simply because they preferred fresh air. Likewise, identical electricity consumption profiles may correspond to entirely different human activities, intentions, or comfort expectations. These latent semantic variables are essential for understanding occupant behavior, yet they are inherently inaccessible to physical sensors. As a consequence, today’s smart buildings are primarily reactive systems. They observe measurable events but have only a partial understanding of the human context that produced them. This limitation reduces the accuracy of activity recognition, occupant modeling, demand prediction, personalized control, and explainable decision-making. More fundamentally, it prevents buildings from continuously learning from their occupants. A truly intelligent building should therefore not only sense its physical environment but also acquire semantic knowledge from its inhabitants. This requires the ability to identify when sensor observations are insufficient, determine which contextual information is missing, interact with occupants at appropriate moments, interpret their responses, and integrate this knowledge into its internal representation of the building state. In other words, a smart building must possess the capability to “listen” to its occupants in the same way that it continuously listens to its sensors. The proposed smart annotation-aiding systems provide this missing capability. Rather than treating annotations merely as offline labels for machine learning, they establish a permanent communication channel through which occupants can explain intentions, goals, preferences, perceived comfort, satisfaction, or exceptional situations that cannot be inferred automatically. Such systems are able to overcome the limitations of previous semi-automated data annotation systems in smart-home energy management: revealing the environmental impact of human behavior, reducing the burden of annotation, and enabling more accurate and personalized decision-making. From this perspective, smart annotation-aiding systems should be considered as a fundamental component of future human-centric buildings. Just as sensors extend a building’s ability to perceive the physical world, annotation-aiding systems extend its ability to perceive the human world. Together, these two complementary information sources enable buildings to continuously improve their understanding of occupant behavior, adapt to changing lifestyles, and support more accurate, explainable, and personalized decision-making. Data annotation is the process of assigning meaningful labels or tags to raw data so that it can be interpreted by machine learning algorithms and used as ground truth for supervised learning (Tan et al., 2024). A data annotation system operationalizes this process through a pipeline that typically includes data collection, label assignment through a defined interface and label schema, and quality control to produce reliable labeled datasets for training and evaluation. Most annotation systems have been designed with the primary objective of generating ground-truth datasets for supervised machine learning. In this traditional paradigm, annotations represent target labels that are used exclusively during the training phase and are subsequently discarded once the predictive model has been learned. While this approach has proven highly successful across numerous domains, it overlooks the substantial amount of contextual and semantic information that annotations may convey. An alternative paradigm consists in considering annotations as informative features rather than solely as supervisory labels. In such annotation-aided intelligent systems, annotations become an integral component of the system state, complementing sensor measurements and other automatically acquired variables. Rather than describing only what occurred, annotations may explain why an event occurred, under which circumstances it occurred, or how it was perceived by the human observer. Consequently, annotations provide access to latent contextual variables that are generally inaccessible through physical sensing alone. The conventional labelling view above is kept only as background: within the proposed framework, an annotation is a time-related semantic input that an occupant supplies about a specific sensor context relevant to an inquiry, not a general-purpose feature attached to an arbitrary record. It complements the sensor-based evidence of a case with the meaning the occupant assigns to a particular interval of time, and it is retained as part of that case for later comparison and reasoning rather than discarded once a predictive model has been trained. This perspective is particularly relevant for cyber-physical systems centred on humans, where many determinants of human behavior remain hidden from conventional sensors. In the context of smart buildings, an occupant annotation such as “I opened the window because the room felt stuffy” contains information about perceived air quality, thermal discomfort, behavioral intention, and adaptive strategies. These semantic concepts cannot be directly inferred from temperature, humidity, or CO2 measurements, yet they constitute valuable explanatory variables for predicting future occupant actions and energy consumption. From a machine learning perspective, annotation-aided systems extend the input space by integrating heterogeneous sources of information, including sensor data, human observations, expert knowledge, textual explanations, and confidence indicators. Recent advances in large language models further facilitate this integration by transforming free-text annotations into structured semantic representations or dense vector embeddings that can be combined with conventional numerical features. Such representations enable predictive models to exploit both quantitative measurements and qualitative human knowledge within a unified learning framework. Data annotation systems are used across many domains, with their design depending on the type of data and task: in Computer Vision, for image classification, object detection, and segmentation, especially in healthcare applications (Sager et al., 2021); in Natural Language Processing (NLP), for tasks such as entity recognition, sentiment analysis, and text classification, increasingly with support from Large Language Models (LLMs); and in Human Activity Recognition (HAR), for ambient sensing and IoT-based smart-home data (Demrozi et al., 2023).This study focuses on an extension of HAR: smart-home energy management. In this domain, sensors can only observe effects, such as power consumption, water usage, door status, or occupancy; they cannot observe the cause behind these effects, namely what the occupant was doing and why. This is because occupant behavior and intent belong to personal experience, not to a physical quantity a sensor can register, no matter how the sensors are configured (del Castillo Cardoso, 2025). Annotation supplies this missing cause: the occupant explains, for example, why a washing machine was started at midnight, or which of several devices sharing the same smart plug (e.g., a TV and a sound system) was actually running.

In addition, deploying annotation in real homes introduces further constraints. Because sensor data are generated continuously, day and night, within the private living space of each household, the annotation system cannot query occupants too frequently, cannot rely on privacy-intrusive sensing, must remain usable by non-expert users, and must adapt to each household’s own routines. However, most existing systems only address part of this problem: they either depend entirely on manual occupant input, or automate labeling without adequately supporting context-aware interaction and selective querying. A number of research directions have proposed partial solutions. Self-experimentation systems address the missingcause problem by letting occupants pose their own questions, explaining their own routines (del Castillo Cardoso, 2025) and revealing their environmental impact. Interactive and cooperative learning approaches instead target occupant burden, by querying occupants selectively and reconciling labels that conflict with sensor evidence (Silva et al., 2022; Awada et al., 2020). These and other related efforts, along with the gap that remains between them, are examined in detail in the following sections. This research develops and evaluates a smart annotation-aiding system: an intelligent human–AI collaboration framework that combines sensor observations with human knowledge to acquire, validate, and exploit semantic information that cannot be automatically sensed. Rather than treating annotation as an end goal, the system treats it as one component of a broader knowledge acquisition process that supports learning, prediction, explanation, and decision making. The core objective is to overcome the limitations of previous semi-automated annotation systems in smart-home energy management by suggesting when to annotate, matching concepts shared between human and machine, and proposing labels to reduce annotation burden. Each of the following objectives targets a specific limitation identified above and in the reviewed literature: 1. Design a goal-based information model: Mapping data labels to candidate sensor data sources and naturallanguage question templates, addressing the lack of a systematic way to determine what information is missing from sensor data and how to request it from the occupants. 2. Build a scenario-aware conversational interface: Integrating a chatbot capable of dynamically making decisions (to ask, defer, or suppress queries) based on sensor evidence and occupant routines, such as silent modes during late nights or absences, addressing systems that either demand manual input for every event or automate labeling without context-aware, selective interaction. 3. Define a declarative configuration schema: Ensuring system flexibility by providing a code-free configuration method, enabling easy scalability to new research domains, addressing the rigidity of existing systems, which are typically built around a fixed set of labels and cannot easily adapt to new households or research domains. 4. Empirically evaluate the framework: Conducting real-world experiments to compare the proposed "AnnoBot" with traditional annotation methods on accuracy, user burden, and privacy preservation, validating whether the design choices above actually deliver the reduction in burden and improvement in label quality they are intended to provide. The study is strictly bounded to residential energy and water consumption analysis, focusing on recognizing occupant behavior and attributing consumption peaks to specific devices or events. This paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews existing annotation systems and related approaches for reducing annotation burden in smart-home and human activity recognition contexts. Section 3 formulates the problem by defining occupant-driven experiments, sensor configurations, temporal horizons, and the annotation system. Section 4 presents the proposed AnnoBot framework and its implementation, including the data collection pipeline, data preprocessing, trigger mechanism, chatbot-based label assignment, and occupant interaction process. Section 5.4 reports the implementation-level results obtained from testing the Telegram-based label assignment module. Finally, Section 6 concludes the paper and discusses future work.
