# Conclusion

[[Annotation aid system index|Index]] | [[05-experiment|Previous]] | [[07-references|Next]]

This paper presented AnnoBot, a smart annotation-aiding system that reframes data annotation for human-centric smart buildings as an occupant-driven inquiry process rather than a step in a machine-learning pipeline. An experiment establishes a time-bounded listening period for one or more inquiries; each inquiry configures the sensors, indicators, detection rule, and annotation scope needed to answer an occupant’s question. Selected sensor traces are collected within that listening period, and a detection rule identifies and bounds a relevant service episode. When the resulting sensor facts answer the inquiry question directly, they already constitute a valid, sensor-based case. Otherwise, the system requests the specific semantic dimensions it is missing: interactive learning decides whether the current context is already sufficiently supported or should trigger a question to the occupant, and, within the scope evaluated in this paper, only the local-neighbourhood-density criterion is active for that decision. Accepted sensor facts and occupant-provided annotations are formalized as first-order-logic assertions sharing a common case identifier, giving the system an explicit, inspectable representation rather than opaque feature vectors. Within this representation, the LLM and the logical reasoner play complementary, bounded roles: the LLM interprets natural language, drives the dialogue, and proposes candidate assertions or explanations, while the logical reasoner checks consistency and completeness; a candidate becomes accepted knowledge only after that symbolic check and/or the occupant’s confirmation. Cooperative learning closes this loop as a repair strategy embedded in quality control: incomplete, uncertain, or conflicting annotations are returned to the occupant for confirmation, amendment, rejection, or deferred resolution, rather than being silently accepted or discarded. Accepted cases can then be compared through the inquiry’s indicators, revealing patterns that the system explains to the occupant, who may revise a routine or test a new hypothesis, restarting the inquiry loop with new evidence. The two illustrations used throughout this paper delimit what has actually been evaluated. The meeting-room inquiry plan demonstrates how one experiment can host several concrete inquiries with distinct recognition paths, but has not yet been deployed. The dishwasher dataset, used to validate the implemented pipeline end to end, gives preliminary, component-level evidence that the system can ingest sensor-event time series, visualize them, and collect interactive semantic annotations through a chatbot interface; it does not constitute evidence of longitudinal household validation or of complete sufficiency decision support. Future work should deploy the framework in real households across both illustrative activities, activate and evaluate the remaining interactive-learning trigger criteria, and assess whether the resulting case base measurably supports occupants in understanding and revising their own energy practices.
