### 2.1. Traditional / Manual Annotation Systems



Manual annotation systems rely entirely on the human to decide when and what to label. A representative example is Label Studio, one of the most widely used open-source labeling platforms, adopted by leading technology organizations such as Meta, NVIDIA, and Intel. Its mechanism follows the manual paradigm: every instance imported into the system is treated as requiring a label, and it is left to the annotator to decide which task to work on and when, with no automatic mechanism determining whether a given instance is actually worth labeling (HumanSignal, Inc., n.d.). This design is

well suited to enterprise settings in the CV or NLP domains, where formally trained annotators are assigned to process a fixed, pre-collected dataset, but it makes such systems difficult to apply directly to the HAR domain, where data are continuous and annotation takes place within the residents’ own homes, by individuals who lack professional labeling experience. In the residential HAR setting, the interface is simplified for non-expert users, and occupants are prompted to label events as they occur in the home. A simple labeling system for elderly individuals used physical push-buttons installed at locations where the corresponding activities typically take place, so that pressing a button directly assigns a label to the ongoing activity (Matsui et al., 2020). This approach is easy to use and well suited to users unfamiliar with technology, but it is limited to predefined activities (bathing, cooking, eating, going out, sleeping, etc.), and label accuracy depends entirely on whether the occupant remembers to press the button at the correct start and end times of the activity. Another study extended this scope further with a recording interface that allowed up to 27 different activities to be recorded, producing 60 to 100 labels per day over a 28-day trial (Alemdar et al., 2013). Although considerably more flexible than fixed push-buttons, this approach still requires the occupant to actively act on every single event, with no mechanism to filter or prioritize the events that genuinely warrant annotation. Taken together, whether general-purpose or simplified for residential use, these systems retain the core mechanism and limitation of the manual category: the decision of when and what to annotate depends entirely on the human, with no support from the system itself. In the context of sensor data generated continuously from the residential environment, this can render the annotation workload excessive in the absence of an intelligent filtering and prioritization mechanism; this is precisely the motivation for the semi-automated systems presented in the following section.



### 2.2. Semi-Automated Systems: Application of Interactive Learning in Annotation System



Interactive learning has been studied as a way to reduce annotation effort by allowing models to selectively use human feedback when labels are uncertain, ambiguous, or costly to obtain. In annotation systems, this paradigm shifts labeling from exhaustive manual annotation toward targeted human intervention. Prior HAR surveys provide useful background for this direction. Bouchabou et al. (2021a) highlight challenges related to sparse sensing, context dependence, and deployment variability, while Hussain et al. (2019) provide a broader taxonomy of sensing paradigms and recognition challenges. Although these works are not centered on annotation systems, they motivate the need for efficient, adaptive, and human-in-the-loop labeling methods.



### 2.3. Methods for Reducing Annotation Burden



Several studies have explored ways to reduce annotation effort in smart-home and activity-related systems. Study (Silva et al., 2022) are the closest to this project’s direction, as they propose a hybrid interactive and cooperative learning framework that combines selective querying, user-defined labels, and confusion repair. Rather than treating annotation as a one-time labeling task, their system frames it as an adaptive dialogue where the model asks for feedback when needed and revisits inconsistent past decisions. Even with user participation, labeling errors, subjectivity, or inconsistencies can still occur. A Cooperative Learning mechanism addresses this by detecting contradictions between a label just provided by the user and the actual sensor measurements, or against previously recorded labels; rather than discarding the data automatically, the system sends a notification suggesting a correction for the user to confirm, amend, or reject (Awada et al., 2020). This two-way mechanism progressively aligns the system’s records with the user’s actual real-world experience. Other works reduce annotation burden by changing how supervision is collected. DISCOVER (Karpekov et al., 2025) clusters recurring sub-activities from unlabeled event streams and asks humans to label representative centroids instead of dense raw sequences. Accoustate (Chatterjee et al., 2021), although not directly designed for ambient smart homes, also reflects the broader concern of reducing manual labeling through modality-assisted auto-annotation. Together, these studies show that annotation-efficiency methods remain fragmented across interactive querying, structure-based label, compression, and assisted auto-annotation. Another related direction focuses on semantic adaptation and cross-home generalization. Bouchabou et al. (2021b) use language-model-based semantic enrichment to represent sensor events beyond raw identifiers. TDOST (Thukral et al., 2024) further converts sensor triggers into textual descriptions, allowing models to transfer across homes with different layouts and sensor vocabularies. DomusFM (Fiori et al., 2026) extends this idea through smart-home foundation-model pretraining across multiple datasets. These works improve deployability under limited labels, but they still do not fully solve the problem of aligning transferred representations with user-specific and evolving labels.

Explainability is also relevant to annotation systems because users are more likely to correct or refine labels when model outputs are understandable. Das et al. (2021) show that explanation design affects user trust in smart-home recognition systems. GNN-XAR (Fiori et al., 2025) incorporates interpretability into a graph-based recognition model, while Fiori et al. (2024) explore the use of large language models to evaluate explanation quality. These studies improve transparency, but they do not directly address when the system should ask for feedback or how conflicting labels should be repaired. Finally, contextual reasoning methods address ambiguity when local sensor evidence is insufficient. TRACE (Shi et al., 2026) is especially relevant because it uses longer-horizon context and user-specific priors to produce more coherent predictions under sparse sensing and domain variation. Compared with Silva et al. (2022), which emphasizes human involvement during annotation, TRACE focuses on reducing ambiguity within the inference process itself. These directions are complementary: effective annotation systems likely require both contextual reasoning to reduce uncertainty and interactive feedback to resolve the uncertainty that remains.



### 2.4. Research Gaps



Across the reviewed literature, the main gap is no longer a lack of awareness that smart-home HAR and annotation are difficult. Existing review papers have already established the core challenges, including sparse sensing, context dependence, multi-user complexity, concept drift, and poor cross-home transferability (Bouchabou et al., 2021a; Hussain et al., 2019). Meanwhile, recent method-oriented studies provide partial solutions from different perspectives, such as reducing annotation effort, improving semantic transfer, enhancing explainability, and strengthening contextual reasoning. However, these directions remain largely fragmented and do not yet form an integrated annotation framework for user-specific, evolving smart-home environments. As shown in Table 1, what is still missing is a unified annotation-centered framework that integrates these strengths into a single human-in-the-loop process. In particular, existing studies still lack: • Integrated annotation workflow: most studies optimize only one aspect of the problem, such as reducing the number of labels, improving transferability, enhancing explanations, or strengthening contextual reasoning, rather than combining these capabilities in one annotation pipeline. • Cooperative ambiguity repair: although user feedback is used in some systems, fewer works support a two-way mechanism for detecting, revisiting, and repairing inconsistent or ambiguous labels over time. • Privacy-preserving interaction: many approaches do not explicitly focus on annotation using non-intrusive ambient environmental and power sensors, which are important for preserving privacy in residential environments.

• User- and home-specific semantic adaptation: transferred or generalized models are not fully aligned with the evolving routines, label meanings, and contextual preferences of individual occupants and homes. These limitations motivate the development of our framework, AnnoBot: a flexible LLM-based chatbot integrated with dialogue policy mechanisms for adaptive, privacy-preserving, and user-centered annotation, as discussed in the following sections.
